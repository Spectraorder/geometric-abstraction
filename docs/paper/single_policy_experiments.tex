\subsection{Single-policy acquisition}
\label{sec:single-policy-experiments}

We test whether geometric abstraction helps an LLM acquire effective manipulation
policies within a small revision budget. The simulated SO-100 performs three
tasks with randomized layouts and colors: Bridge + cylinder, ordered three-cube
stacking, and hexagonal ring-on-peg placement
(Figure~\ref{fig:single-policy-task-illustrations}).

\paragraph{Comparison and procedure.}
Base and Full share the task specification, calibrated RGB-D access, and action
interface. Base can implement perception in its program; Full additionally
receives sensor-grounded geometry, geometric queries, and textual feedback.
Appendix~\ref{app:geometric-abstraction} describes their construction.
Neither condition can access simulator object truth or private evaluator state.
Both use \texttt{gpt-6-astra} with high reasoning effort.

For each task, we run five paired training chains, each with five shared training
layouts and separate persistent LLM contexts. Starting from an observation-only
scaffold (R0), the LLM generates three policy versions (R1--R3). Each version runs
on all five layouts, with a scene reset between rollouts and no LLM calls during
execution. Outcomes and observations inform the next revision. A candidate is
retained only if it succeeds on every previously successful training layout and
at least one additional layout; otherwise the incumbent remains.

After all training freezes, every saved version is tested on the same 100 held-out
layouts per task. Physical success requires the target arrangement, release, and
stability. Test results never inform revisions or retention.
Figure~\ref{fig:single-policy-curves} shows mean test success of the
training-retained policies and cumulative tokens through each iteration across
five chains.
Table~\ref{tab:single-policy-results} in
Appendix~\ref{app:single-policy-details} gives the per-revision results.

\paragraph{Findings.}
Geometric abstraction helps acquire effective policies earlier on the two
construction tasks. Full's largest advantage appears at R1. By R2, its mean
test SR exceeds Base's R3 on both tasks, with cumulative token use approximately
half of what Base has spent through R3. The gap narrows as Base improves, while
Full retains higher final mean success. At the same final revision, token totals
are similar on Bridge and lower on Stacking.

Ring follows a different pattern: both have zero retained test SR through R2,
and Full reaches 38\% at R3 while Base remains at zero.
Thus, Ring supports successful acquisition within the same revision budget,
rather than the earlier-success pattern of the construction tasks. These gains
combine policy synthesis with geometric assistance during execution; they do not
isolate faster LLM inference or text serialization alone.
