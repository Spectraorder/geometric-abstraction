\subsection{Continuous online reasoning and execution}
\label{sec:online-experiments}

We next examine geometric abstraction when the language model makes decisions
within a continuing episode. Unlike single-policy acquisition, this setting
requires the agent to ground successive actions in the scene produced by its
previous execution. We study three tasks: constructing a bridge with two cubes
stacked above its center, placing a ring over a fixed peg, and relocating a
preassembled cube column onto a separate foundation. These separately
registered studies each use five fresh paired layouts, one episode per condition
and layout, and a 200-second wall-clock budget. Episodes reset only at
initialization; unsuccessful actions leave their consequences in the scene.
There are no injected disturbances or replacement episodes.

\paragraph{Agent and shared experience.}
Base and Full use persistent \texttt{gpt-6-astra} contexts with medium reasoning
effort and share task specifications, public robot information, and action
interfaces within each task. Tower supplies adapted bridge and stacking skills;
the agent chooses construction order and explicit support-surface targets. Ring
supplies the same trained grasp-and-lift skill to both conditions, selected using
training outcomes alone. This skill uses sensor geometry in both conditions and
returns control before the trained placement routine executes. The agent then
chooses finger-pad targets, wrist orientation, observations, and release. Thus,
Ring evaluates online decisions with matched local grasp assistance, rather than
an end-to-end geometry-free Base. Shared controllers execute submitted targets
without substituting task-specific destinations. Skills make no language-model
calls internally.
Relocation supplies shared one-placement skills without a relocation plan. The
agent selects temporary placements, explicit targets, and recovery actions to
move the upper two cubes onto a new foundation. Shared local perception measures
one table-supported cube and transfers its dimensions under the public equal-size
assumption; every current pose still requires visual evidence. Both foundations
must be restored to their initial positions within 5\,mm during the final
five-second stable hold. This local assistance is shared by Base and Full.


\paragraph{Observation and timing.}
Both conditions can request images and calibrated pixel-depth samples. Base
receives image and qualitative feedback; Full receives compact current geometric
references with evidence and timestamps, retaining initial and requested images.
Unavailable estimates remain explicit. Runtime inputs exclude simulator object
truth and private evaluator state. The comparison evaluates supplied perception,
geometric references, and feedback together, rather than text serialization alone.
The episode clock includes initialization after reset, sensing, model waits,
actions, and settling. Physics advances during model waits. Tower success requires
the prescribed support structure and five seconds of stability; Ring requires
released placement around the peg with two seconds of stability. Both conditions
use the same private criterion within each task.

\paragraph{Measures.}
We report success within budget and mean capped completion time,
$N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\min(T_j,200)$, with $T_j=\infty$ for timeouts and $N=5$
per condition. Model time measures end-to-end request wall time, including service
waiting, rather than isolated inference compute. We also record request counts,
tokens, image attachments, and local execution cost. Token totals include cached
input once. Unknown usage from incomplete requests is reported separately.

\paragraph{Results and interpretation.}
Table~\ref{tab:online-results} shows task-dependent benefits. On Tower, Full
completed four episodes versus Base's two and reduced mean capped time by 11.1\%.
Full succeeded on three layouts where Base timed out, but timed out on one where
Base succeeded. Full submitted fewer placements (22 versus 27) and attached fewer
images (20 versus 33), while recorded tokens increased by 6.3\%. Mean completed
request duration increased from 6.42 to 7.07 seconds. The completion advantage
therefore does not imply uniformly faster or cheaper model reasoning.

On Ring, both conditions succeeded on all five layouts, with Full completing
every paired layout sooner. Mean completion time decreased by 38.0\%, cumulative
completed model time by 55.7\%, and recorded tokens by 60.2\%. Completed requests
fell from 63 to 33, while mean request duration fell from 7.49 to 6.34 seconds;
image attachments fell from 62 to 10. Full used more local camera acquisitions
(123 versus 87) and more accumulated action time. The result is consistent with
additional local geometric processing reducing repeated model interaction, rather
than reducing every component of execution cost. Ring's interrupted Base request
ended when the scene satisfied the success criterion; its token usage remains
unknown.

On Relocation, Full completed three episodes versus Base's two, reducing mean
capped time by 13.8\%, completed model time by 37.8\%, and recorded tokens by
16.7\%. Completed requests fell from 57 to 41 and image attachments from 55 to 20.
Physical action time was similar (537.26/551.51 seconds Base/Full across five
episodes), while Full acquired more camera frames. Full gained two successes
where Base timed out and lost one where Base succeeded. Each arm has two
interrupted requests with unknown usage; including their durations yields mean
cumulative model times of 80.03/50.55 seconds per episode.

These small studies illustrate complementary uses of geometric abstraction: supporting
physical decisions under a deadline and supplying actionable state with fewer
model interactions. They evaluate task-specific skill reuse on new layouts,
not learning from scratch or generalization to arbitrary objects. Five pairs
without repeated trials do not separate model/service variability from layout
variation or establish a general success-rate advantage. Earlier online pilots
also produced equal-success and mixed-cost outcomes. We therefore interpret the
results as exploratory evidence about when the interface helps, rather than a
universal latency or token-efficiency claim.
